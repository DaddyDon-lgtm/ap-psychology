1Start Here: What the AAQ Is

The AAQ is FRQ 1 on the AP Psychology exam: one summarized, peer-reviewed study, six parts, and 7 points. It is worth 16.65% of your total exam score, about the same as 25 multiple-choice questions.

The six parts
PartWhat you doPoints
AIdentify the research method1
BState an operational definition1
CDescribe what a statistic means in the study1
DIdentify an ethical guideline and describe how it was applied1
EExplain whether the findings are generalizable1
FExplain how a finding supports or refutes a hypothesis or concept2

Time: 25 minutes, including a 10-minute reading period. The order and wording of the parts can change slightly from year to year, but these six skills are the core.

Map the source before you write

AAQ sources usually have four sections, and each one feeds certain parts.

Section of the studyWhat it gives youParts
IntroductionThe hypothesis or concept being testedF
ParticipantsWho was studied, and often ethicsE, D
MethodThe design, how variables were measured, and ethicsA, B, D
Results and DiscussionNumbers, tables, graphs, and the findingsC, F
Your 10-minute reading routine
  1. Read the six prompts first, so you know what you are hunting for.
  2. Label each section of the study with the part letters it helps with.
  3. Circle the variables that were measured.
  4. Highlight signal words: “randomly assigned,” “measured,” “scored,” “consent,” “correlated,” “significant.”
  5. Star the result you would use for Part F.

Want to practise this? Try Map the Source near the end of the page.

2Rules for Every Part

Readers score each part on its own, so every part must stand alone and answer exactly what was asked.

  • Write in complete sentences. Lists, bullet points, and fragments do not score.
  • Label every part (A, B, C…) so the reader can find each answer fast.
  • Answer the command word. “Identify” means name it. “Describe” means say what it means. “Explain” means give the reason, using evidence.
  • Use the source. The answer is almost always in the text. Use the study’s numbers, groups, and wording.
  • Define briefly, then apply. College Board: “A definition alone will not earn the point, but a clear definition can support the application.” Keep the definition accurate: a wrong definition that contradicts your answer can cancel the point.
  • Commit to one answer. Two methods, two guidelines, or “both supports and refutes” without evidence for each side counts as contradicting yourself.
  • Never write “the researchers should have…” Readers score what the study did, not what it should have done.
  • Don’t claim cause unless the study is an experiment. In a correlational study, use “tended to,” “was related to,” or “predicted,” never “caused” or “proved.”

Part AResearch Method

Name one research method from the five the course lists, in one sentence. That earns the point.

Prompt: Identify the research method used in the study.1 point

Sentence frame

“The research method used in this study is ______.”

The five possible answers
MethodClues in the sourceSignal words
ExperimentParticipants randomly assigned to groups or conditions; researchers manipulate an independent variable; experimental and control (or placebo) groupsrandomly assigned, placebo, control group, condition
CorrelationalTwo or more variables measured as they already exist; researchers look for a relationship; often reports rrelated to, associated with, predicts, correlated
Case studyOne person or a small group studied in depth, often because of something rarean alias (“H.M.”), the participant, the subject
Naturalistic observationBehavior watched in a natural setting with no interferenceobserved, recorded, hidden cameras, natural setting
Meta-analysisResults combined from many earlier studies; no new participantsstudies were included, pooled, excluded studies
Trap: survey is not a research method

A survey or questionnaire is how the data were collected. If researchers used a survey to look for a relationship between variables, the method is correlational.

Laboratory observation is a real research method, but it isn’t one of the five AAQ answer options. On the exam, translate it into one of the five: if researchers only watched behavior, answer naturalistic observation; if they changed something and measured the effect, answer experiment.

Trap: comparing groups is not an experiment

If the groups already existed (age, grade, gender, AP score) and nobody was randomly assigned, it is not an experiment. And commit to one method: “an experiment or a correlational study” does not earn the point.

The experiment with no control group

Some experiments use a within-subjects design: every participant experiences every condition. There is no separate control group. Instead there is a control condition (the normal or baseline condition), and each participant serves as their own control.

The source may never say “within-subjects,” “control group,” or even “random assignment.” It might only say that the order of conditions was mixed up or balanced. Use the key test: did the researchers change something and measure the effect? If yes, the answer is still experiment.

Example

Twenty students each took a reading test twice, once in a silent room and once with music playing. Half did the silent room first, and half did the music first. This is an experiment. The silent room is the control condition, and balancing the order does the job random assignment does in a group design.

Name That Method Try it

Pick the research method for each study. Feedback tells you which clue to look for.

1. College students drew numbers to decide whether their phone stayed on the desk or in another room during a reading test.

2. Researchers gave 600 teenagers a questionnaire about daily social media use and loneliness and reported r = +.29.

3. An observer sat unnoticed in a school cafeteria for two weeks and recorded how often students ate alone.

4. Every participant completed a typing test once in a warm room and once in a cold room. Half did the warm room first.

5. A neurologist wrote a detailed report on one man who lost the ability to form new memories after surgery.

6. A team combined the results of 48 published studies on exercise and anxiety and reported an overall effect size.

7. Researchers compared the reading motivation scores of existing 3rd, 5th, and 8th graders.

Score it

“The research method is an experiment because the researchers compared 3rd graders to 8th graders.”

“The researchers used a survey.”

“The research method used in this study is correlational research.”

Part BOperational Definition

State how this study measured the variable, in a way someone could count or score. The source always holds the answer.

Prompt: State the operational definition of [variable].1 point

Sentence frame

“The operational definition of ______ is the participants’ ______ on ______.”

Textbook (conceptual) definition

Sleepiness: feeling a strong need to sleep.

Says what the idea means. On its own, earns 0.

Operational definition

Sleepiness: a person’s rating from 1 to 9 on a sleepiness scale.

Says how the study measured it. Earns the point.

A brief textbook definition is fine as a lead-in, as long as the measurement follows. Look in the Method section for “measured,” “rated,” “scored,” “completed,” “coded,” and “higher scores meant.”

Operational definitions don’t have to be numbers

  • A score on a scale: “The operational definition of self-esteem is the participant’s score on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale.”
  • Speed or accuracy on a task: “The operational definition of executive functioning is how quickly participants correctly identified whether the second set of items was the same as or different from the first.”
  • Coded categories (practice example): “The operational definition of emotional overeating is the participant’s interview responses that describe eating in response to negative emotions.”
Trap: the wrong variable

Most studies measure more than one variable. Make sure your definition matches the variable named in the prompt. In the motivation study, GPA is the operational definition of achievement, not of motivation.

Score it

“Intrinsic motivation is when you do something because you enjoy it.”

“The operational definition of intrinsic motivation is the student’s GPA.”

“The operational definition of intrinsic motivation is the student’s average rating, from 1 to 5, on the 17 intrinsic statements in the questionnaire.”

Part CInterpreting a Statistic

Say what the number means for the people in this study. Restating the number, or defining the statistic with no link to the study, earns 0.

Prompt: Describe what [statistic] indicates about [variable or groups] in the study.1 point

Sentence frames

Means: “The means show that the ______ group ______ more than the ______ group.”

Correlation: “The correlation of ___ shows that people who ______ tended to ______.”

Spread: “The standard deviation shows that scores in the ______ group were more / less spread out than in the ______ group.”

What each statistic means
StatisticWhat it tells youHow to say it in a study
MeanThe average score of a group“The placebo group recalled fewer words on average.”
MedianThe middle score when scores are put in order; less affected by extreme scores than the mean“Half of the participants were older than 34.”
ModeThe most common score“The most common age was 34.”
RangeHighest score minus lowest score“Scores in the control group were more spread out.”
Standard deviationHow much scores vary around the mean; larger SD means more spread out“Recall scores varied more after staying up late.”
SkewA lopsided distribution. Positive skew: a tail of high scores pulls the mean above the median. Negative skew: a tail of low scores pulls the mean below the median.“A few very high incomes pulled the mean above the median.”
Percentile rankThe percentage of scores at or below this score“H.S. scored higher than about 95% of test takers.”
Correlation (r)Direction (+ or −) and strength (closer to ±1 is stronger, closer to 0 is weaker) of a relationship“Students with higher intrinsic motivation tended to have higher GPAs.”
Statistical significanceThe result is unlikely to be due to chance“The difference was unlikely to be due to chance.”
Effect sizeHow big a difference or relationship is. For Cohen’s d, about 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large“Sleep had a medium-sized effect on memory.”
What each research method usually reports
MethodStatistics you usually seeTypical graph
ExperimentGroup or condition means and SDs; significance; sometimes effect sizeBar graph of group means, or a line graph over time
Correlationalr values; sometimes means or percentages for groups that already existedScatter plot, a table of r values, or bars for existing groups
Meta-analysisNumber of studies and participants; an overall effect size or rTable or forest plot of study results
Case studyOne person’s scores compared with typical people; percentile ranksTable of scores
Naturalistic observationCounts, frequencies, percentages; sometimes the modeBar graph or table of how often each behavior happened

What Does r Look Like? Try it

Drag the slider and watch 120 students’ scores change. Then press the buttons to see the real values from the motivation study.

StrengthModerate

Simulated students. Strength labels follow the usual guide: below .30 weak, .30 to .49 moderate, .50 and above strong. For more, see Statistics 3: Correlation and Regression.

Traps

A negative r is not “weaker.” Strength is distance from zero, so r = −.32 is stronger than r = +.27.

A correlation never shows cause. “Extrinsic motivation causes lower grades” earns 0. Use “tended to.”

Restating is not interpreting. “The correlation is −.23” earns 0 on its own.

Score it

“The correlation between extrinsic motivation and GPA is −.23.”

“The negative correlation shows that extrinsic motivation causes lower grades.”

“The correlation of −.23 shows that students who reported more extrinsic motivation tended to have somewhat lower GPAs.”

Part DEthical Guidelines

The ethics point needs two moves: name a guideline the source describes and say what these researchers did to apply it. Naming alone earns 0.

Prompt: Identify one ethical guideline described in the study. Describe one way the researchers in the study applied this ethical guideline.1 point

Sentence frame

“The researchers followed the ethical guideline of ______ when they ______.”

The guideline menu
GuidelineWhat it meansWhat it looks like in a source
IRB approvalAn institutional review board (ethics committee) approved the study before it began“approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board”
Informed consentParticipants (or parents, for minors) agree after learning what the study involves“participants agreed to take part,” “with parental consent”
Informed assentParticipants under 18 agree for themselves“children agreed to participate”
Protection from harmResearchers reduce physical or emotional risk“researchers considered factors that might impose difficulty”
ConfidentialityParticipants’ identities are protected“participants could not be identified from the data”
Right to withdrawParticipants can quit at any time“knew they could back out at any time”
DebriefingParticipants learn the study’s true purpose afterward“participants were told the purpose after the study”
Traps

Only what the source describes. You may know a guideline that should apply. If the source does not say the researchers did it, it does not count.

No “should have.” “The researchers should have kept answers confidential” earns 0.

Design features are not ethics. Random assignment, a large sample, or a standardized survey are not ethical guidelines.

Minors: consent comes from a parent or guardian. Assent from the child only counts if the source mentions it.

Score it

“The study used informed consent.”

“The researchers protected the children from harm because filling out a questionnaire is not dangerous.”

“The researchers followed the ethical guideline of informed consent when they got permission from the students’ parents before the students completed the questionnaire.”

Part EGeneralizability

Name a population and back your claim with a feature of the participants that the source reports. “Generalizable” and “not generalizable” both earn the point when the evidence fits.

Prompt: Explain the extent to which the research findings may or may not be generalizable using specific and relevant evidence from the study.1 point

Sentence frame

“The findings are / are not generalizable to ______ because the participants were ______.”

Counts: participant features the source reports

  • Age or grade level
  • Location (city, region, country)
  • Type of school, community, or setting
  • Ethnicity or cultural background
  • Gender or sex
  • How participants were selected

Does not count

  • Sample size (“797 is a big sample”)
  • Reliability or validity of the measures
  • Confounds
  • Guesses about personality, character, motivation, or effort
  • “Everyone is different”
  • A claim with no population named

A trait like personality counts only if the source reports it as a participant characteristic, for example as a selection criterion.

Trap: the “partly” rule

If you say “partly generalizable,” or both generalizable and not, give evidence for each side. One side only earns 0.

Does It Count? Try it

Each item is a piece of evidence a student used for Part E in the motivation study. Does it count?

“All participants were in grades 3 through 8.”

“There were 797 students, which is a big sample.”

“Both school districts were in the San Francisco Bay Area.”

“Some of the students might have been lazy.”

“42% of participants were Asian American and 34% were White.”

“The questionnaire might not be valid.”

“One district was known for high academic achievement.”

Score it

“The study is generalizable because it had 797 students.”

“The findings may not be generalizable, because some students might have been lazy.”

“The findings may not be generalizable to high school students, because all of the participants were in grades 3 through 8.”

“The findings may apply to both boys and girls, since the sample was about half each, but may not apply to students outside California, since both districts were in the San Francisco Bay Area.”

Part FSupport or Refute

Part F is worth 2 points: use one specific result from the study, explain how it connects to the hypothesis or concept, and interpret it accurately.

Prompt: Explain how at least one of the research findings supports or refutes [the hypothesis or concept].2 points

Sentence frame

“The finding that ______ supports / refutes the hypothesis that ______ because ______.”

How Part F is scored
PointsWhat the response does
2A specific result, explained clearly, connected to the hypothesis, and interpreted accurately
1A result with no explanation, OR an explanation with no result, OR a result interpreted with an error (such as claiming cause from a correlation)
0No accurate connection between the results and the hypothesis
Three steps to 2 points
  1. Pick one specific result: a number, a table value, a graph comparison, or a stated finding.
  2. Say whether it supports or refutes the hypothesis.
  3. Explain the link in plain words: what does the result mean for the people in the study?
Traps

A number with no explanation, or an explanation with no result, caps you at 1 point. “Proves” or “causes” in a correlational study is an inaccurate interpretation. Saying it both supports and refutes needs evidence for each side.

Score it: 0, 1, or 2?

“Intrinsic motivation had a correlation of +.34 with GPA.”

“The hypothesis is supported because students who like learning do better in school.”

“Intrinsic motivation was positively correlated with GPA (r = +.34), which proves that enjoying school causes higher grades.”

“The finding that intrinsic motivation was positively correlated with GPA (r = +.34) supports the hypothesis, because students who reported enjoying challenging schoolwork tended to earn higher grades.”

A “refutes” example

Suppose the prompt asked about the claim that working for rewards and teacher approval improves achievement. A 2-point answer: “The finding that extrinsic motivation was negatively correlated with test scores (r = −.32) refutes the claim, because students who worked mainly for the teacher or to avoid hard work tended to score lower, not higher.”

9Practice: Map the Source

This short practice source is fictional. For each sentence, pick the part it helps you answer. This is exactly what your 10-minute reading period is for.

Phones and Focus Try it

Introduction

Researchers predicted that having a phone in sight would lower students’ reading comprehension.

Participants

Participants were 120 first-year students, aged 18 to 20, at one university in Taipei.

All students signed a consent form and were told they could stop at any time.

Method

A computer randomly assigned each student to keep their phone face-down on the desk or to leave it in another room.

Reading comprehension was measured as the number of correct answers, out of 20, on a quiz about a passage students read.

Results and Discussion

Students with the phone on the desk scored a mean of 13.1 correct (SD = 3.2), compared with 15.4 (SD = 2.9) for students whose phone was in another room.

10Worked Example 1: Does Loving School Pay Off?

This is the correlational study from our AAQ workshop, with a full 7-point response. The complete source is in the Canvas quiz “AAQ Workshop: Motivation and Achievement.”

Source: adapted from Lepper, M. R., Corpus, J. H., & Iyengar, S. S. (2005). Intrinsic and extrinsic motivational orientations in the classroom: Age differences and academic correlates. Journal of Educational Psychology, 97(2), 184–196.

  • Hypothesis: students with higher intrinsic motivation would have higher academic achievement.
  • Participants: 797 students in grades 3 to 8 from two public school districts in the San Francisco Bay Area; 401 girls and 395 boys; 42% Asian American, 34% White, 5% Hispanic, 2% African American, 10% other, 7% not reported; parental consent.
  • Method: students rated 17 intrinsic and 13 extrinsic statements from 1 (not at all true for me) to 5 (very true for me). Achievement was GPA (suburban district) or California Achievement Test scores (urban district).
  • Results: intrinsic motivation fell from a mean of 4.07 (SD = 0.67) in grade 3 to 3.42 (SD = 0.75) in grade 8. All four correlations below were statistically significant.
GPA (n = 216)Test scores (n = 136)
Intrinsic motivationr = +.34r = +.27
Extrinsic motivationr = −.23r = −.32
Show the 7-point model response

(A) The research method used in this study is correlational research.

(B) The operational definition of intrinsic motivation is the student’s average rating, from 1 to 5, on the 17 intrinsic statements in the classroom questionnaire.

(C) The correlation of −.23 shows that students who reported higher extrinsic motivation tended to have lower GPAs, although the relationship was fairly weak.

(D) The researchers followed the ethical guideline of informed consent when they obtained consent from the students’ parents before the students completed the questionnaire.

(E) The findings may not be generalizable to students in other countries, such as Taiwan, because all of the participants attended public schools in the San Francisco Bay Area of California.

(F) The finding that intrinsic motivation was positively correlated with GPA (r = +.34) supports the hypothesis that students with higher intrinsic motivation have higher academic achievement, because students who rated statements like “I like hard work because it’s a challenge” as more true for them tended to earn higher grades.

11Worked Example 2: The College Board Sample

The sample AAQ in the AP Psychology Course and Exam Description is an experiment: about 3,500 adults over 60 were randomly assigned to take a daily multivitamin or a placebo, and their memory and executive functioning were tested once a year for 3 years. The multivitamin group’s immediate recall improved by a mean of 0.71 words after one year, compared with 0.44 words for the placebo group. The examples below are College Board’s own.

Official scored examples
PartEarns the pointDoes not earn
A“The research method is an experiment.”“The research is a correlational study.”
B“The operational definition is the scores on the executive functioning test.”“Executive functioning is how many words they recall.” (wrong variable)
C“The means show that the multivitamin group had better recall than the placebo group.”“The mean is the average of a group of data.” (definition only)
D“Researchers maintained confidentiality when they ensured that the individual participants could not be identified from the data collected.”“The study was approved by an IRB and the researchers debriefed their participants.” (not described in the study)
E“The study is generalizable to older adults only because that is the only group the researchers used.”“The study is generalizable to the larger population.” (no evidence)
F (2)“This study shows that older adults can delay some cognitive decline if they take a multivitamin because it seems to improve episodic memory, but not executive functioning.”“Older adults gain 3.1 years of memory change because of taking a multivitamin.” (1 point: result, no explanation)

Compare the two examples. Example 1 is correlational, so its answers say “tended to.” Example 2 is an experiment with random assignment, so cause-and-effect language is allowed.

Self-score checklist

Tick each part after a practice AAQ. Your ticks are saved on this device only.

The five costliest mistakes
  1. Defining without applying (Parts B and C): a definition alone earns 0.
  2. Restating instead of interpreting (Part C): “r = −.23” is not an answer.
  3. Naming without describing (Part D): the ethics point needs both.
  4. Using sample size or guesses for generalizability (Part E).
  5. Claiming cause from a correlation (Parts C and F).
