Describing Data

Mean, median, mode, and range, and when each one lies to you

1Why Summarize At All

A researcher finishes a study and has 200 numbers. Nobody can hold 200 numbers in their head, so we replace them with a few numbers that describe the whole set. Those are descriptive statistics.

Two questions do most of the work:

  • Where is the center of the data? That is mean, median, and mode.
  • How spread out is the data? That is range and standard deviation.

The trap is that every one of these summaries throws information away. That is the point of a summary. But it means each one can hide something important, and the AP exam likes to ask which one is hiding something.

3The Three Measures of Center

Mean: the arithmetic average. Add every score, divide by how many scores there are. The mean uses every single data point, which is its strength and its weakness. One extreme score changes it.

Median: the middle score when the data is put in order. Half the scores are above it, half below. If there is an even number of scores, take the average of the middle two. The median only cares about position, not size, so an extreme score barely moves it.

Mode: the score that appears most often. A data set can have no mode, one mode, or more than one mode. Mode is the only one of the three that works on data that is not numerical, such as favorite color.

Use theWhen
Meanthe data is roughly symmetric with no extreme outliers
Medianthe data is skewed or has outliers, which is why income is always reported as a median and never a mean
Modeyou need the most common category, or the data is not numerical
The income example, because it is the clearest

Put nine ordinary people in a room. Their mean income and median income are close. Now walk in a billionaire. The mean income of the room is now over a hundred million dollars, which describes nobody standing there. The median barely moved, and it still describes the room honestly.

5Range, and Why It Is a Weak Measure



Range is the highest score minus the lowest score. It is the simplest measure of variability and the least informative, because it uses only two numbers and ignores everything in between.



These two data sets have an identical range of 40:




  
SetScoresRange

  
    
A30, 48, 50, 51, 52, 54, 7040

    
B30, 32, 35, 50, 65, 68, 7040

  




Set A is tightly clustered with two stragglers. Set B is genuinely spread out. Range cannot tell them apart. Standard deviation can, which is the next page.



6AP Exam Tips



    
  
  • When a question describes skewed data and asks which measure of center best represents it, the answer is the median.
    • 
  
  • When a question gives you a data set and asks for the median, put the numbers in order first. Students lose this mark constantly by taking the middle number of an unordered list.
    • 
  
  • If a distribution has an even number of scores, the median is the average of the two middle values, and it may not be a value that appears in the data at all.
    • 
  
  • A data set can have no mode. If nothing repeats, "no mode" is the correct answer, not zero.
    • 
  
  • Range is a measure of variability, not of center. Questions sometimes offer it as a distractor when asking about central tendency.
    •

  Question 1
  
A psychology class takes a quiz. Eight students score between 78 and 85. One student, who was ill, scores 12. Which measure of central tendency would best represent the typical performance of the class?

  
Which measure should the teacher report?

  
    
    
  1. The mean, because it uses every score
    2. 
    
  2. The median, because it is not distorted by the extreme low score
    3. 
    
  3. The mode, because it shows the most common score
    4. 
    
  4. The range, because it shows the full spread
    5. 
  

  
  

    
Answer: B

    
The score of 12 is an outlier. Because the mean uses every value, that one score drags it well below what the rest of the class actually did. The median only cares about position, so it still sits among the scores in the 78 to 85 band and describes the class honestly.

    
    
      
  • A is wrong: using every score is exactly the problem here.
    • 
      
  • C is plausible but weaker: the mode only tells you the most repeated score, which may be a coincidence in a set this small.
    • 
      
  • D is wrong: range measures spread, not center.
    • 
    

  






  Question 2
  
A researcher reports that the mean of a data set is 62 and the median is 48.

  
What does this tell you about the shape of the distribution?

  
    
    
  1. It is symmetric
    2. 
    
  2. It is negatively skewed, with a tail of low scores
    3. 
    
  3. It is positively skewed, with a tail of high scores
    4. 
    
  4. It is bimodal
    5. 
  

  
  

    
Answer: C

    
The mean is pulled toward the tail, because the mean is the only measure of center that responds to how far away a score is. A mean sitting well above the median means high values are doing the pulling, so the tail stretches to the right, which is positive skew.

    
    
      
  • A is wrong: in a symmetric distribution mean and median are about equal.
    • 
      
  • B has the direction backwards: a low-score tail would pull the mean below the median.
    • 
      
  • D is wrong: nothing here indicates two peaks.
    • 
    

  






  Question 3
  
Five students report the number of hours they slept: 6, 7, 7, 8, 22.

  
Which statement is accurate?

  
    
    
  1. The mean is 7 and the median is 10
    2. 
    
  2. The mean is 10 and the median is 7
    3. 
    
  3. The mean and median are both 7
    4. 
    
  4. There is no mode
    5. 
  

  
  

    
Answer: B

    
The mean is 50 divided by 5, which is 10. In order, the middle of five scores is the third, which is 7. The 22 is almost certainly a recording error, and it has pushed the mean three hours above what any typical student reported.

    
    
      
  • A reverses the two values.
    • 
      
  • C ignores the effect of the outlier on the mean.
    • 
      
  • D is wrong: 7 appears twice, so 7 is the mode.
    • 
    

  






  Question 4
  
Two classes take the same test. Both have a range of 30 points.

  
What can you conclude about the two distributions?

  
    
    
  1. They have the same variability
    2. 
    
  2. They have the same mean
    3. 
    
  3. Their highest and lowest scores differ by the same amount, and nothing more
    4. 
    
  4. Both distributions are normal
    5. 
  

  
  

    
Answer: C

    
Range is built from exactly two numbers, the maximum and the minimum. Everything between them is invisible to it. One class could be tightly bunched with two stragglers while the other is evenly spread, and both would report the same range.

    
    
      
  • A overstates it: identical range does not mean identical variability, which is the reason standard deviation exists.
    • 
      
  • B does not follow: range says nothing about center.
    • 
      
  • D does not follow: range says nothing about shape.
    •
