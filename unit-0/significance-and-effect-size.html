1The Two Questions

When a study reports a difference between two groups, two separate questions need answering, and each has its own statistic.

The questionThe statisticWhat it tells you
Is this difference real, or could chance alone have produced it?Statistical significance, reported as a p-valueHow surprising the result would be if there were no effect
How big is the difference, and would anyone notice it?Effect sizeThe size of the difference in practical terms

A study can answer yes to the first and still be useless, because the answer to the second was "barely any." Modern research reports both, and the AP exam expects you to know why one cannot substitute for the other.

3Statistical Significance and p-Values

Statistical significance means a result is unlikely to have happened by chance alone. By convention, researchers call a result significant when the p-value is below .05, meaning there is less than a 5% probability of seeing a difference this large if there were really no effect at all.

Read that definition carefully, because the order matters

The p-value answers "how surprising would this result be if there were no effect?"

It does not answer "what is the probability that there is no effect?" Those sound similar and are not the same statement. A p-value describes the data given a hypothesis, not the hypothesis given the data.

Two things significance does not mean, and both are tested:

  • It does not mean the effect is large. With a big enough sample, a tiny and useless difference becomes statistically significant.
  • It does not mean the finding is important, or that it will replicate, or that the study was well designed. A significant result from a badly confounded experiment is still a badly confounded experiment.

Effect size answers the question significance cannot: how big is it?

Two things to try

Set the true effect to zero and run it. Some studies still come out significant. That is what a 5% false positive rate looks like, and it is why replication matters.

Set a tiny effect with a huge sample. Almost everything becomes significant even though the effect is trivial. That is the "significant but meaningless" problem made visible.

5AP Exam Tips

  • Statistical significance means unlikely due to chance. It does not mean large, and it does not mean important.
  • Effect size indicates the strength of a relationship or the size of a difference. Roughly: 0.2 and below small, 0.3 to 0.7 medium, 0.8 and above large.
  • A large sample can make a trivial difference statistically significant. When a question contrasts a huge sample with a tiny difference, it is testing this.
  • A p-value tells you how surprising the data would be if there were no effect. It does not tell you the probability that the finding is true.
  • Significance says nothing about whether the study was well designed. A confounded experiment can produce a significant result.

6Practice Questions

Answer each one before you reveal it.

Question 1

A study of 40,000 participants finds that a new study technique improves test scores by an average of 0.4 points out of 100, and the result is statistically significant at p < .001.

What is the best interpretation?

  1. The technique has a large effect on learning
  2. The result is unlikely to be due to chance, but the effect is far too small to matter practically
  3. The result must be a coding error, since p is so small
  4. The study proves the technique causes better learning in all students

Answer: B

A very large sample makes even minuscule differences detectable, which is what the tiny p-value reflects. Significance answers "is this likely to be real," not "is this big enough to care about." A 0.4 point gain out of 100 is not worth changing anyone's study habits over. This is exactly why effect size is reported alongside significance.

  • A confuses statistical significance with magnitude.
  • C misunderstands p-values: a small p is expected with a huge sample.
  • D overreaches on generalization even if the design were experimental.
Question 2

Two therapies for anxiety are compared. Therapy A shows an effect size of 0.25 and Therapy B shows an effect size of 0.9. Both results are statistically significant.

What does this tell a clinician?

  1. Both therapies work equally well, since both are significant
  2. Therapy B produces a substantially larger difference and would likely matter more to a patient
  3. Therapy A did not really work
  4. Therapy B is significant and Therapy A is not

Answer: B

Both effects are real, since both are significant. Effect size tells you how much they differ in magnitude. An effect size of 0.9 is large, while 0.25 is small, so patients would notice considerably more benefit from Therapy B.

  • A is the error the question is built around: significance is not a measure of size.
  • C is wrong: a small effect that is statistically significant is still a genuine effect, just a modest one.
  • D contradicts the stem, which says both are significant.
Question 3

A researcher reports p = .03.

Which statement is the most accurate reading of that number?

  1. There is a 3% chance that the researcher's hypothesis is wrong
  2. If there were really no effect, there would be about a 3% chance of getting a result at least this extreme
  3. The effect accounts for 3% of the difference between groups
  4. The finding will replicate 97% of the time

Answer: B

A p-value is calculated by assuming there is no effect and then asking how surprising the observed data would be under that assumption. It describes the data given a hypothesis, not the hypothesis given the data.

  • A reverses that logic. It is the most common misreading of p-values among students and, frankly, among a lot of published researchers.
  • C describes something closer to a proportion of variance, which is a different statistic.
  • D is unsupported. Replication depends on effect size, sample size, and study quality, not on the p-value from a single study.
Question 4

Two studies test the same treatment. Study A uses 30 participants and reports a large effect size that is not statistically significant. Study B uses 3,000 participants and reports a small effect size that is statistically significant.

What is the most reasonable conclusion?

  1. Study B is better because its result was significant
  2. Study A is better because its effect size was larger
  3. The two studies disagree, so the treatment does not work
  4. Neither study alone settles it, and the results are worth combining in a meta-analysis

Answer: D

Small samples make significance hard to reach even when an effect is real, so Study A's non-significant result is weak evidence of absence rather than evidence of no effect. Large samples make significance easy to reach even when an effect is trivial. The two statistics are measuring different things, which is why combining results across studies is the standard response to exactly this situation.

  • A treats significance as a quality rating rather than a function of sample size.
  • B ignores that a large effect size from 30 people is an unstable estimate.
  • C misreads the situation: the studies do not actually contradict each other.
