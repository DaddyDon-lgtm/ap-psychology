3Reading the Correlation Coefficient

The correlation coefficient, written r, is a single number from -1.00 to +1.00 that carries two separate pieces of information.

The sign gives direction. Positive means the two variables rise and fall together. Negative means as one rises, the other falls.

The absolute value gives strength, meaning how close to a straight line the points lie. Near 1.00 either way is strong, and one variable predicts the other well. Near 0 is weak, and knowing one tells you almost nothing about the other.

So r = -0.85 describes a much stronger relationship than r = +0.40.

5Correlation Still Is Not Causation

A slider makes relationships feel mechanical, so it is worth restating the limit here.

The directionality problem: even if two variables are genuinely related, a correlation cannot tell you which one came first. Does stress cause poor sleep, or does poor sleep cause stress?

The third-variable problem: an unmeasured variable may be causing both. Ice cream sales and drowning rates rise together because both are caused by hot weather.

The research methods page has the full treatment, including why only an experiment with random assignment licenses a causal claim.

6Regression Toward the Mean

When a measurement comes out extreme, the next measurement of the same thing tends to land closer to average. Nothing has changed. The extreme was partly produced by chance, and chance does not repeat on demand.

Think of any score as two things added together: real ability, plus luck on the day. A student who scores 98 almost certainly has real ability, and also had a good day. The ability carries over to the next test. The good day does not. So the second score drifts down toward their true level. The same logic runs in reverse for a student who scores 31.

This is not a psychological effect. It is arithmetic, and it happens whenever the same thing is measured twice and the two measurements are less than perfectly related.

The connection to r, which is why it sits on this page

How much regression you get is decided by the correlation between the two measurements.

  • r = 1.00: the two measures agree perfectly, so there is no regression at all.
  • r = 0.60: extreme scores move part of the way back toward the mean.
  • r = 0.00: the second score is unrelated to the first, so extreme groups fall all the way back to the overall average.

Weaker relationship, more regression. That is the whole rule, and the widget below lets you watch it happen.

8Why This Fools Everyone

Regression toward the mean is dangerous because it produces evidence that looks exactly like a treatment working.

Interventions almost always start when things are at their worst. A student is tutored after a bad test. A patient seeks treatment when symptoms peak. A team fires the coach after a terrible run. Then things improve, and the intervention gets the credit it may not have earned.

The classic case

Flight instructors noticed that when they praised a cadet for an unusually smooth landing, the next landing was usually worse. When they shouted at a cadet for a rough landing, the next one was usually better. They concluded that criticism works and praise backfires.

They were watching regression toward the mean. An unusually good landing is followed by a more ordinary one, and an unusually bad landing is followed by a more ordinary one, whatever the instructor says. The instructors had taught themselves a false lesson about human behavior from a pattern that was pure arithmetic.

Other places it shows up:

  • The athlete who has a spectacular rookie season and a disappointing second one.
  • The miracle cure that reaches people at rock bottom and takes credit for the recovery that was coming anyway.
  • The school that adopts a new program after its worst year on record.
The fix, and it is always the same

A control group of people who were equally extreme to begin with and received nothing. If the treatment group improves more than they do, the improvement is real. Without that comparison, you cannot separate a working treatment from ordinary regression.

9AP Exam Tips

  • The sign of r is direction, the absolute value is strength. r = -0.85 is stronger than r = +0.40.
  • A correlation of any size never establishes causation.
  • When reading a scatterplot, name the direction first, then judge the strength from how tightly the points cluster around a line.
  • Whenever a group is selected because its scores were extreme and then improves, regression toward the mean is a live alternative explanation. Ask whether there was a control group of equally extreme people.
  • The weaker the correlation between two measurements, the more regression you get between them.

10Practice Questions

Answer each one before you reveal it.

Question 1

Study A reports r = +0.35 between two variables. Study B reports r = -0.78 between two different variables.

Which statement is accurate?

  1. Study A found the stronger relationship because the value is positive
  2. Study B found the stronger relationship because 0.78 is further from zero than 0.35
  3. The two relationships are equally strong
  4. Study B found a weaker relationship because the value is negative

Answer: B

Strength is the distance from zero, in either direction. 0.78 is further from zero than 0.35, so Study B's relationship is considerably stronger. The negative sign tells you only that as one variable rises the other falls.

  • A and D both treat the sign as though it measured strength, which is the single most common error with correlation coefficients.
  • C ignores the difference in magnitude.
Question 2

Four scatterplots are shown to a class.

Which pattern indicates the strongest relationship?

  1. Points scattered evenly across the whole plot with no direction
  2. Points loosely trending upward with wide vertical spread
  3. Points clustered tightly along a line sloping downward
  4. Points forming a horizontal band across the middle

Answer: C

Strength is about how tightly points hug a straight line, not which way the line tilts. A tight downward cluster is a strong negative correlation, close to -1.

  • A describes r near 0.
  • B describes a weak to moderate positive correlation: there is a direction, but wide spread means poor prediction.
  • D also describes r near 0, since the value of one variable does not change as the other varies.
Question 3

A school identifies the 25 lowest-scoring students on a September reading test and enrolls them in an after-school program. In January they retest, and the group's average has risen by 9 points. The principal concludes the program works.

What is the strongest objection?

  1. Nine points is not a statistically significant improvement
  2. The group was selected for extreme low scores, so some improvement was expected through regression toward the mean alone
  3. Reading scores cannot be measured reliably
  4. The sample of 25 is too small to analyze

Answer: B

The students were chosen precisely because they scored at the bottom, and an extreme low score is partly a bad day. On any retest, that group drifts upward toward their true level whether or not anything is done for them. To claim the program caused the gain, the school needs a comparison group of equally low scorers who received no program.

  • A cannot be judged from the information given, and significance is not the issue here.
  • C is unsupported and too sweeping.
  • D is a weak objection: 25 is workable, and sample size is not what makes this conclusion unsafe.
Question 4

A coach records each player's free throw percentage in game one and again in game two. The correlation between the two games is r = 0.30. The five players who shot best in game one shoot considerably worse in game two.

What best explains this?

  1. The players became complacent after their strong first game
  2. The correlation is negative, so good performance predicts bad performance
  3. The two measurements are weakly related, so extreme scores regress substantially toward the mean
  4. Free throw shooting is not a real skill

Answer: C

With r = 0.30 the two games are only weakly related, which means a large part of any single game's result is chance. The players selected for being extreme in game one give back most of that luck in game two. The lower the correlation between two measurements, the more regression you see between them.

  • A is the tempting psychological story, and it is exactly the kind of false explanation regression invites. It requires no evidence and explains nothing that arithmetic does not already explain.
  • B misreads the coefficient: 0.30 is positive.
  • D is unsupported. Skill is real; it is simply not the only thing determining a single game.
