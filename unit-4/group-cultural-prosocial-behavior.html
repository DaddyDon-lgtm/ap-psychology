3Audiences and Crowds

Social facilitation and social inhibition

Being watched raises arousal, and arousal strengthens whatever response is most likely for you (your dominant response). If you have mastered a task, your dominant response is the right one, so an audience helps. If the task is hard or new, your dominant response is often wrong, so an audience hurts.

Social facilitation

Easy or well-practiced task + audience = better.

An experienced pianist plays her best at the recital.

Social inhibition

Hard or new task + audience = worse.

A beginner who plays fine alone stumbles when classmates watch.

Study tip that falls straight out of this research: practice a new skill alone until it is solid, then perform it in front of others. By then the audience works for you.

Social loafing

Imagine a tug-of-war. If one person pulls at 100 percent, how hard does each person pull in a group of eight? In classic studies, blindfolded people who believed others were pulling with them gave only about 82 percent of their solo effort. When individual contributions cannot be identified, effort quietly drops. Sound like any group project you have been part of? The fix is accountability: make each person's contribution visible.

Deindividuation

When we feel anonymous and aroused in a crowd (at a concert, a protest, behind a screen name), self-awareness and self-restraint loosen, and people may do things they never would alone. Masks, uniforms, darkness, and anonymous accounts all increase it. It fuels riots and online mobs, but it can also be joyful, like losing yourself in a stadium singing together.

4How Groups Decide

Group polarization

When like-minded people discuss an issue, their shared view gets stronger and more extreme, not more moderate. You and your friends are mildly annoyed about a new school rule; after twenty minutes of agreeing with each other, you are furious. Online echo chambers supercharge this. The antidote is real contact with people who see things differently.

Groupthink

Groupthink happens when a group's desire for harmony overrides a realistic look at the alternatives. Doubters stay silent, the group feels falsely confident, and warning signs get ignored. Irving Janis coined the term after studying the failed 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion; other decisions blamed on groupthink include the lack of preparation at Pearl Harbor and the 1986 Challenger launch.

How to prevent groupthink
  • Assign a "devil's advocate" whose job is to argue against the plan.
  • Have leaders welcome dissent and hold back their own opinion early.
  • Invite outside experts to critique the plan.

After the Bay of Pigs, President Kennedy deliberately invited disagreement during the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the outcome was far better.

The false consensus effect

We overestimate how many people share our opinions and habits: "everyone thinks this," "most people would do the same." It feeds both polarization and groupthink, because if you believe everyone already agrees, you never question the group.

5Social Traps, Shared Goals, and the Workplace

A social trap is a situation where each person, sensibly pursuing their own interest, helps create harm for everyone. Each person drives alone for convenience, and everyone sits in gridlock. Each fishing boat catches all it can, and the fishery collapses. Climate change and antibiotic resistance are social traps on a global scale.

Superordinate goals are the opposite: shared goals that no group can reach alone. In Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave study, two rival groups of boys at a summer camp became hostile through competition. The hostility faded only when the groups had to work together to fix a broken water supply and move a stuck supply truck.

Industrial-organizational (I/O) psychology puts this unit to work: it studies hiring, training, motivation, leadership, and teamwork in organizations, including how to reduce social loafing and groupthink on real teams.

6Culture

Culture is the set of behaviors, ideas, values, and traditions a group shares and passes on: the food at your table, the rules about eye contact and personal space, how loudly you talk on the MRT. Like water to a fish, your own culture is mostly invisible until you meet a different one. Cultures also change over time through immigration, technology, and contact.

Two separate dimensions of culture
DimensionOne endOther end
How strictly are norms enforced?Tight: clear rules, strongly enforced. Waiting for the "walk" signal at an empty intersection.Loose: flexible rules, tolerated rule-bending. Jaywalking is common and ignored.
Whose goals come first?Individualist: personal goals, independence, "I." Many Western cultures lean this way.Collectivist: group goals, harmony, belonging, "we." Many Asian, African, and Latin American cultures lean this way.

Multiculturalism means multiple cultural groups coexist within one society and their distinct identities are recognized and valued. It contrasts with assimilation, where minority cultures are expected to blend into the dominant one. Think about Taiwan: where would you place it on each dimension, and how has it changed over your parents' and grandparents' lifetimes?

7Prosocial Behavior: When We Help

Prosocial behavior is any action meant to benefit others. Altruism is helping with no expectation of personal gain, sometimes at real cost to yourself.

The bystander effect

The more people who witness an emergency, the less likely any one of them is to help. Research on this began after the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York. The original newspaper story claimed 38 neighbors watched and did nothing; that account turned out to be heavily exaggerated, and some neighbors did try to help. But the story sent psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané into the lab, where they showed the effect in controlled experiments: participants who believed they were the only one hearing someone having a seizure helped far more often and faster than those who believed others were listening too.

The main reason is diffusion of responsibility: with others present, each person feels less responsible ("someone else will handle it"). We are most likely to help when we notice the event, interpret it as an emergency, and assume responsibility.

Use this if you ever need help

Do not shout "someone call for help!" to a crowd. Point to one person and give them a job: "You, in the red jacket, call 119." Assigning responsibility to one person defeats diffusion of responsibility. (In Taiwan, 119 is for ambulance and fire, 110 for police.)

8Don't Mix These Up

Social facilitation vs. social inhibition vs. social loafing

Facilitation

Audience + easy task = better.

Inhibition

Audience + hard task = worse.

Loafing

Working with a group, effort drops because no one can tell who did what.

The tell: facilitation and inhibition are about being watched. Loafing is about pooled work where individual effort is hidden.
Social loafing vs. deindividuation

Social loafing

Less effort.

One member of the group project does almost nothing.

Deindividuation

Less self-restraint, from anonymity and arousal.

Anonymous accounts pile hateful comments on a stranger.

The tell: slacking off is loafing. Doing something you normally would not do, under cover of a crowd or anonymity, is deindividuation.
Group polarization vs. groupthink

Group polarization

The group's existing view gets more extreme after discussion.

Groupthink

The desire for harmony silences doubts, producing a bad decision.

The tell: look for "more extreme after discussion" (polarization) versus "no one spoke up, and the plan failed" (groupthink).
Social trap vs. superordinate goal

Social trap

Self-interest creates shared harm.

Superordinate goal

A shared goal forces cooperation.

The tell: they are opposites. A trap ends badly for everyone; a superordinate goal brings rival groups together.
Tight/loose vs. individualist/collectivist

Tight / loose

How strictly norms are enforced.

Individualist / collectivist

Whose goals come first: mine or the group's.

The tell: these are two separate dimensions. A scenario about punishing rule-breakers is tight/loose. A scenario about family duty versus personal ambition is individualism/collectivism.
Altruism vs. social exchange vs. the two norms

Expects something back

Social exchange (rewards outweigh costs) and the reciprocity norm (repaying past help).

Expects nothing back

Altruism (unselfish, often costly) and the social-responsibility norm (helping those who cannot repay).

The tell: ask whether the helper expects anything in return, and whether the person being helped has helped them before.

11AP Exam Traps

Watch for these
  • Facilitation vs. inhibition depends on the task, not the audience. Same crowd, opposite effect.
  • Loafing = less effort. Deindividuation = less self-restraint. Do not mix them up.
  • Group polarization makes views more extreme, not more moderate. Groupthink is about silenced dissent and bad decisions.
  • Tight/loose and individualism/collectivism are separate dimensions.
  • The bystander effect: more people, less help. The mechanism to name is diffusion of responsibility.
  • Altruism expects nothing back. Social exchange and reciprocity both involve a return.
  • Find the trigger in each scenario: an audience, anonymity, like-minded discussion, suppressed dissent, number of bystanders, or an expected reward.
