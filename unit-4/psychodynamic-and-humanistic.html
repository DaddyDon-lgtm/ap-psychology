1Start Here

Personality is your characteristic pattern of thinking, feeling, and behaving. Psychologists explain it through four lenses. This page covers the two oldest and most provocative: the psychodynamic view, where hidden forces steer you, and the humanistic view, where you are driven to grow. Topic 4.5 covers the other two, the social-cognitive and trait views.

  • How do we protect ourselves from anxiety without realizing it?
  • What conditions help a person grow toward their full potential?

3Freud and the Unconscious

Sigmund Freud developed psychoanalysis, both a theory of personality and a method of therapy, while treating patients whose physical symptoms had no medical cause. His central claim was unsettling: much of who you are operates below your awareness. ("Psychoanalytic" and "psychodynamic" are often used interchangeably; modern psychodynamic psychologists keep the unconscious but drop many of Freud's specific claims.)

The mind as an iceberg

  1. Conscious: what you are aware of right now. The tip above the water.
  2. Preconscious: not in awareness now, but easy to recall, like your phone number.
  3. Unconscious: hidden wishes, fears, and memories you cannot easily reach, which Freud argued steer your behavior. Most of the iceberg.

Freud believed the unconscious leaks out. In free association, a patient relaxes and says whatever comes to mind so buried material can surface. He called dreams "the royal road to the unconscious," and he read slips of the tongue ("Freudian slips") as hidden wishes breaking through.

4Id, Ego, and Superego

Freud divided personality into three systems in constant negotiation.

SystemOperates onVoice
IdThe pleasure principle. Unconscious, present from birth, wants immediate gratification."I want it now."
SuperegoMorality. The internalized conscience from parents and society; can produce guilt."That's wrong. You should be better."
EgoThe reality principle. The mediator that finds realistic ways to satisfy the id that the superego can accept."Let's find a workable way."
The bubble tea test

You promised yourself no more sugary drinks this week, and you walk past your favorite bubble tea shop. Id: "Large, full sugar, extra pearls, right now." Superego: "You made a commitment. Giving in means you have no willpower." Ego: "Not today. I'll get one Saturday with friends, half sugar."

Background only: psychosexual stages

Freud also proposed that children pass through psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital) and that getting "stuck" at a stage (fixation) shapes adult personality. You will hear these terms in pop culture, but they are not a focus of the current AP exam.

5Defense Mechanisms

When impulses or painful realities threaten to overwhelm us, Freud said the ego protects itself, unconsciously, by distorting reality. Like cognitive biases, defense mechanisms operate automatically; we rarely catch ourselves using them.

MechanismWhat the ego doesExample
RepressionPushes anxiety-arousing thoughts and memories out of awareness. Freud saw it as the basis of the others.An adult has no memory of a frightening childhood event.
DenialRefuses to accept a painful reality.A heavy smoker insists the health risks are "overblown."
RegressionRetreats to an earlier, more childlike stage.A stressed teenager starts sucking his thumb or throws a tantrum.
RationalizationInvents self-justifying explanations to hide the real reasons."I didn't get the part because the director plays favorites."
ProjectionAttributes your own unacceptable feelings to others.A student who wants to cheat accuses everyone else of cheating.
Reaction formationActs the opposite of your true feelings.Being overly sweet to a coworker you secretly resent.
DisplacementRedirects an impulse onto a safer target.Yelling at your little brother after your coach yells at you.
SublimationChannels unacceptable impulses into a socially valued activity.Turning aggressive urges into success as a boxer.

A neo-Freudian idea: the collective unconscious

Freud's followers, the neo-Freudians, accepted the unconscious but revised much of his theory. The one you need is Carl Jung, who proposed the collective unconscious: a shared, inherited reservoir of images and memory traces from our species' history. Jung used it to explain why similar myths, symbols, and fears (the hero, the flood, the monster in the dark) show up across cultures that never met.

6Assessing the Unconscious: Projective Tests

Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli and ask people to respond. With no clear "right answer," the theory goes, hidden needs and conflicts slip out.

Rorschach inkblot test

People describe what they see in ten symmetrical inkblots.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

People make up stories about ambiguous pictures of people and scenes.

Critics argue projective tests have weak reliability (different scorers reach different conclusions) and weak validity (they do not predict behavior well). Defenders see them as a way to open conversation in therapy.

Data interpretation practice

Researchers compared inter-scorer agreement (how often two trained scorers reach the same conclusion) for two tests. Representative values: MMPI (objective trait inventory): 0.85. Rorschach (projective): 0.45. The best-supported conclusion is that the MMPI is scored far more consistently, meaning it has higher reliability. It would be an overreach to say the Rorschach cannot be scored reliably "at all": 0.45 is moderate, not zero. You will meet the MMPI in Topic 4.5.

Freud's scorecard

What did not hold up: many specific claims are hard or impossible to test, and the theory often "explains" behavior only after the fact. Freud built it mostly from case studies of a small number of his own patients, many of them wealthy women in Vienna, which limits generalizability and cannot establish cause and effect. What lasted: the idea that much of mental life is unconscious, that childhood shapes us, and that we defend ourselves against anxiety, plus a vocabulary ("ego," "denial," "Freudian slip") woven into everyday language.

7The Humanistic Theory

By the mid-1900s, some psychologists rejected both Freud's focus on dark, hidden conflicts and behaviorism's view of people as products of conditioning. Humanistic psychology focuses instead on healthy people, free will, and our built-in drive to grow. Its two best-known figures are Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers.

Maslow: the self-actualizing person

Abraham Maslow studied healthy, fulfilled people, such as Abraham Lincoln and Eleanor Roosevelt, and described them as self-actualizing: living up to their full potential. He is famous for his hierarchy of needs, which the CED places in Topic 4.6, Motivation. For personality, the key idea is the upward drive toward becoming your best self.

Rogers: the person-centered view

Carl Rogers believed people are like plants: given the right conditions, they naturally grow toward their potential. He called this the self-actualizing tendency. Like a potato sprouting toward a sliver of light in a dark cellar, a person pushes toward growth even in poor conditions.

The most important growth condition is unconditional positive regard: accepting and valuing a person no matter what they do or reveal. Rogers added genuineness (being open and honest) and empathy (truly understanding the other person's feelings). A parent who says "I'm disappointed in what you did, but I love you no matter what" is showing unconditional positive regard.

The self-concept

For both Maslow and Rogers, the center of personality is the self-concept: all your thoughts and feelings in answer to "Who am I?" Rogers held that we thrive when our self-concept is realistic and accepted, and we struggle when there is a wide gap between who we are and who we think we should be.

Beyond the core: real self and ideal self

Rogers described a real self (who you believe you are) and an ideal self (who you wish you were). When they closely match (congruence), people tend to feel healthy and free to grow. A wide gap (incongruence) is linked to anxiety and low self-worth. Unconditional positive regard helps people accept their real self and shrink the gap.

Evaluating the humanistic approach. It renewed interest in healthy growth, the self, and free will, and it shaped modern counseling and later positive psychology. Critics say its concepts, like self-actualization, are vague and hard to measure, and that its focus on the self can encourage self-centeredness.

8Don't Mix These Up

Displacement vs. sublimation

Displacement

Impulse goes to a safer target. Usually still harmful.

Kicking the dog after a bad day at work.

Sublimation

Impulse goes into a socially valued activity.

Channeling anger into hours at the boxing gym.

The tell: ask whether the result is productive and admired (sublimation) or just aimed at someone less threatening (displacement).
Projection vs. reaction formation

Projection

"You have the feeling I can't accept in myself."

A jealous boyfriend accuses his girlfriend of being jealous.

Reaction formation

"I act the opposite of what I feel."

Showering an unwanted new sibling with exaggerated affection.

The tell: projection moves the feeling onto someone else. Reaction formation flips the feeling into its opposite.
Rationalization vs. denial

Rationalization

Admits the event happened, but gives a self-justifying excuse.

"I only failed because the test was unfair."

Denial

Refuses to accept that the reality exists.

"I don't have a gaming problem," after gaming 10 hours a day.

The tell: rationalization explains the problem away. Denial says there is no problem.
Unconditional positive regard vs. the self-actualizing tendency

Unconditional positive regard

An attitude others give you: total acceptance.

Self-actualizing tendency

Your own inner drive to grow.

The tell: if the scenario describes how a counselor, parent, or friend treats someone, it is unconditional positive regard. If it describes the person's own push to grow, it is the self-actualizing tendency.
Projective tests vs. objective inventories

Projective (psychodynamic)

Ambiguous stimuli, open responses: Rorschach, TAT. Weaker reliability.

Objective (trait, Topic 4.5)

Standard questions, scored the same way every time: MMPI, Big Five inventories. Stronger reliability.

The tell: the MMPI is not a projective test, even though it is used in clinical settings.

11AP Exam Traps

Watch for these
  • Match the principle to the structure: id = pleasure principle, ego = reality principle, superego = morality.
  • Tell the close mechanisms apart: displacement (safe target) vs. sublimation (productive outlet); projection (others have my feeling) vs. reaction formation (I act the opposite).
  • Defense mechanisms are unconscious. A person deliberately choosing to vent at the gym to calm down is coping, not a defense mechanism in Freud's sense.
  • Projective tests (Rorschach, TAT) are the psychodynamic tool, and their reliability and validity are weak. The MMPI is not projective.
  • Collective unconscious = Jung. Psychosexual stages are background only.
  • Humanistic = growth and free will. Know unconditional positive regard (Rogers) and self-actualization (Maslow and Rogers). Maslow's hierarchy belongs to Motivation.
  • On free-response prompts, apply the exact mechanism: name the impulse and where it goes in the scenario.
