1Start Here

Every time you see someone do something, your brain answers a question you never asked out loud: why did they do that? This topic is about how we answer that question, the predictable ways we get it wrong, and how our expectations and comparisons bend what we see in other people and in ourselves.

  • How do we decide whether someone's behavior comes from who they are or from the situation they are in?
  • Why are we so generous explaining our own failures and so harsh explaining everyone else's?
  • How do our expectations of people quietly shape the way those people actually behave?

3Person Perception and Attribution

Your brain writes a biography before they say a word

Person perception is how we form impressions of other people: their traits, their intentions, and what they are likely to do next. We do it fast, automatically, and often before anyone speaks. First impressions form within seconds and are surprisingly hard to shake, because we fill the gaps with assumptions drawn from past experience, culture, and media.

That speed is useful. Nobody can stop to gather full data on every stranger on the MRT. But speed costs accuracy, and the costs are real: who gets hired in the first minute of an interview, whose idea gets taken seriously in a group project, which student a teacher assumes already understands. The judgment feels like observation. It is actually interpretation.

Heider's big question: the person or the situation?

Attribution theory (Fritz Heider, 1958) says we explain behavior by crediting one of two kinds of cause.

Dispositional (internal)

The behavior reflects who they are: personality, attitude, ability, effort.

"She failed the test because she's lazy."

Situational (external)

The behavior reflects where they are: circumstances, pressure, luck.

"She failed because she was sick all week and the test was brutal."

The behavior is identical. The explanation is a choice, and that choice drives how we feel about the person and how we treat them. A driver cuts you off: "reckless jerk" makes you angry; "maybe they're rushing someone to the hospital" lets it go.

Culture shapes the default

People raised in more individualist cultures (often Western) lean toward dispositional explanations: "it's who they are." People raised in more collectivist cultures (often East Asian, including Taiwan) pay more attention to the situation and context. The "obvious" explanation depends partly on the culture you learned to explain things in.

4When Attribution Goes Sideways: Three Biases

Fundamental attribution error

When explaining other people's behavior, we overestimate their disposition and underestimate their situation. The waiter is rude, so "he's a rude person," not "he has twelve tables and hasn't had a break in five hours."

Why it happens: when you watch someone act, the person is front and center on your mental screen. The deadlines, bad news, and rough morning pushing on them are off-screen. The easiest explanation to reach is "that's just how they are." Psychologist Lee Ross named it "fundamental" in 1977 because he thought it sits underneath so many everyday misjudgments.

Self-serving bias

We credit our successes to ourselves and blame our failures on the situation. Aced the test: "I'm smart and I studied." Failed the test: "The teacher writes trick questions." It protects self-esteem by quietly rewriting the scoreboard so we always look good. You see it in sports interviews and company reports all the time: wins come from "our preparation," losses from "the referees" or "market conditions."

Actor-observer bias

We explain our own behavior situationally, but other people's identical behavior dispositionally. The explanation flips depending on whose shoes you are in. You trip on a cracked sidewalk: "that crack is dangerous." Someone else trips on the same crack: "klutz."

5Explanatory Style and Locus of Control

Explanatory style: "I'm doomed" vs. "I'll fix it"

Explanatory style is your habitual way of explaining what happens to you, broadly optimistic or pessimistic. It runs along three dimensions: internal vs. external (my fault, or outside forces?), stable vs. unstable (permanent, or temporary?), and global vs. specific (affects everything, or just this one thing?).

Two students, one failed math test
DimensionAlex (pessimistic)Jamie (optimistic)
Internal / external"I'm not smart enough.""That test was really hard."
Stable / unstable"I'll never get math.""I didn't study enough this time."
Global / specific"I'm a failure at everything.""I'm doing fine in my other classes."
Likely resultGives upStudies differently and stays motivated

A pessimistic style is linked to lower motivation, learned helplessness, and depression. An optimistic style supports resilience. Notice that Jamie still takes some responsibility ("I didn't study enough"), but frames it as temporary and fixable.

Locus of control: who's holding the steering wheel?

Locus of control (Julian Rotter) is a person's general belief about how much control they have over the outcomes in their life.

Internal locus of control

"My choices and effort shape what happens to me."

Failed an exam: "I'll change how I study."

External locus of control

"Luck, fate, and other people decide my outcomes."

Failed an exam: "The teacher has it out for me. Why bother?"

An internal locus is generally linked to better grades, health, and persistence. You will meet locus of control again when we study personality, stress, and health.

6Perception in Action

The mere exposure effect

Repeated exposure to something (a song, a face, a brand, an idea) tends to increase how much we like it, even with no real interaction and no new information. The song you skipped in September is your favorite by November. The classmate you had no opinion about becomes a friend just by sitting near you all semester. Advertisers repeat the same jingle until the brand simply feels trustworthy, and in elections, name recognition alone moves votes. The effect works below awareness, so you usually cannot feel it happening.

The self-fulfilling prophecy

A self-fulfilling prophecy is an expectation that causes itself to come true. It always runs as a loop:

  1. You form an expectation about someone. It may be completely wrong.
  2. You act differently toward them because of that expectation.
  3. Your behavior nudges them to act in line with your expectation, which "confirms" it.

The classic example is Rosenthal and Jacobson's Pygmalion study. Teachers were told that certain students were "intellectual bloomers" about to take off academically. In fact those students had been chosen at random. Expecting more, teachers gave them more attention, warmth, and challenging work, and by the end of the year those students showed greater gains. The expectation manufactured its own evidence.

Research methods link

Because the "bloomers" were selected at random, the only systematic difference between them and their classmates was what the teachers believed. That is exactly why researchers use double-blind procedures: an experimenter's expectations can change participants' behavior.

Mirror-image perceptions

In a conflict, both sides often hold matching, opposite views: each sees itself as reasonable and peaceful and the other as hostile and untrustworthy. Rival schools, feuding friend groups, and opposing governments all say the same thing about each other: "We're the reasonable ones. They started it." Because each side reads the other's defensive moves as proof of hostility, the conflict feeds itself.

7Social Comparison and Relative Deprivation

Measuring ourselves against others

Social comparison (Leon Festinger) is judging our own abilities, achievements, and traits by comparing them to other people's. We rarely judge ourselves in a vacuum.

  • Upward comparison: measuring against someone better off. It can motivate ("I want to get there") or discourage ("I'll never get there").
  • Downward comparison: measuring against someone worse off. It can boost self-esteem, or breed complacency.

Social media is a comparison machine: you compare your ordinary Tuesday to everyone else's highlight reel. That is almost always an upward comparison against a curated version of reality, which is one reason heavy social media use is linked to lower mood and self-esteem.

Relative deprivation: how a raise can make you feel poorer

Your manager surprises you with a $100 bonus, and you feel great. On your way out, you overhear a coworker doing the same job say they got $400. Suddenly the same $100 feels like an insult. Nothing about your situation changed. Only the comparison did.

That is relative deprivation: feeling deprived because of a comparison, not because of an actual loss. It explains a strange pattern in whole societies too: people can become objectively better off and still feel more discontented when the people around them are advancing faster.

8Don't Mix These Up

These are the pairs the AP exam loves to put side by side as answer choices.

Fundamental attribution error vs. actor-observer bias vs. self-serving bias

FAE

One judgment of another person that ignores their situation.

A fan decides a player who missed a shot "has no mental toughness."

Actor-observer

Me vs. you for the same behavior.

"I missed because of the glare. You missed because you choke."

Self-serving

My win vs. my loss.

"I scored because I'm skilled. I missed because of the glare."

The tell: if the scenario compares two different people, think actor-observer. If it compares one person's success and failure, think self-serving. If there is only one judgment of someone else, think FAE.
Explanatory style vs. locus of control

Explanatory style

How you explain events after they happen, along three dimensions (internal/external, stable/unstable, global/specific).

"I failed because I'm hopeless at everything." (internal, stable, global)

Locus of control

A general belief about how much control you have over future outcomes.

"There's no point trying. It's all luck anyway." (external)

The tell: look for the three dimensions (permanent? everything?) for explanatory style. Look for "can I change this?" for locus of control. They often travel together, so read the question to see which one it asks about.
Dispositional attribution vs. internal locus of control

Dispositional attribution

An explanation of one behavior (anyone's) as caused by traits.

"He cheated because he's dishonest."

Internal locus of control

A person's belief about themselves: my effort controls my outcomes.

"If I practice more, I'll make the team."

The tell: both use the word "internal," which is the trap. Attribution is about explaining a behavior; locus of control is about believing you can steer your own life.
Mere exposure effect vs. self-fulfilling prophecy

Mere exposure

Your liking changes because of repetition alone.

You grow to love a song the café plays every day.

Self-fulfilling prophecy

Someone else's behavior changes because of how your expectation made you treat them.

A coach expects a player to be lazy, gives her less playing time, and she stops trying.

The tell: mere exposure needs repetition with no new information. A self-fulfilling prophecy needs the full loop: expectation, changed behavior, confirmation.
Social comparison vs. relative deprivation

Social comparison

The act of measuring yourself against others, upward or downward.

Checking your classmates' scores to see how you did.

Relative deprivation

The negative feeling of being worse off that can follow an upward comparison.

Feeling like a failure with a B+ once you learn your friends all got A's.

The tell: if the scenario stresses that nothing actually got worse but the person now feels worse, the answer is relative deprivation.

11AP Exam Traps

Watch for these
  • Separating the three biases is the most common 4.1 question. Use the filter: whose behavior, win or loss, compared to whom?
  • Wording swaps. The exam may say "internal" instead of "dispositional," or "external" instead of "situational." They mean the same thing in attribution questions.
  • Mere exposure means no new information. If the scenario adds good experiences with the person or thing, it is not pure mere exposure.
  • A self-fulfilling prophecy needs the loop. If the expectation does not change anyone's behavior, it is not a self-fulfilling prophecy.
  • Comparison is the act; relative deprivation is the feeling. Name the direction (upward or downward) and connect it to the emotional result.
  • On free-response "apply" prompts, never just define. Name the person, the behavior, and the attribution, then explain the direction of the bias in that scenario.
  • Stay in 4.1. Prejudice, stereotypes, in-group bias, and the just-world phenomenon belong to Topic 4.2. Do not use them in a 4.1 answer unless the prompt asks.
