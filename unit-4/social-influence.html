1Start Here

Most people believe they would never go along with an obviously wrong group, obey an order to hurt someone, or fall for a sales trick. The research on this page says most people are wrong about themselves. This topic is about the pressure other people put on us, whether it comes from the group beside us, an authority above us, or a message designed to change our minds.

  • Why do people go along with a group, even when the group is clearly wrong?
  • How far will ordinary people go when an authority tells them to?
  • What makes a message, or a request, persuasive?

3Norms and Roles

Social norms are a group's rules for how members should behave. Most are unspoken: we queue for the MRT, lower our voices in the library, and face forward in an elevator, though no one ever taught us these as rules. You notice a norm most clearly the moment someone breaks it. The discomfort you feel is the norm working.

A role is the set of norms attached to a position: student, customer, team captain, employee. Stepping into a new role often feels like acting at first. A new teacher feels like a fraud; a new team captain feels awkward giving instructions. Then, gradually, the role becomes real. What we do, we slowly become.

4Conformity

Conformity is adjusting our behavior or thinking to match a group. Some of it is automatic: the chameleon effect is our tendency to unconsciously mimic other people's expressions and postures. We yawn when others yawn and laugh when others laugh.

Sherif: how norms are born

Muzafer Sherif (1936) used an optical illusion: a still point of light in a dark room appears to move. Alone, people gave very different estimates of how far it moved. In groups, their estimates converged on a shared number, and each person kept using the group's number later, even alone. Groups create norms, and once formed, those norms stick inside us.

Asch: conforming to the obvious wrong answer

Solomon Asch (1951) asked people to match a line to one of three comparison lines, an easy task with an obvious answer. The other "participants" were actors who confidently gave the same wrong answer. Real participants went along with the group's clearly incorrect choice about one-third of the time. Alone, people made errors less than 1 percent of the time.

We conform more when we
  • feel incompetent or insecure
  • are in a group of at least three people
  • face a unanimous group (a single ally breaks the spell)
  • admire the group's status
  • have made no prior public commitment
  • are being watched, especially in a culture that strongly values fitting in

Why we conform: two pressures

Normative social influence

We conform to be liked and avoid rejection, even when we privately disagree.

Laughing at a joke you did not find funny. Dressing like your friend group.

Informational social influence

We conform because we think others know better.

Following the crowd to the exit in an unfamiliar building. Checking reviews before you buy.

Quick test: "I want them to like me" is normative. "They must know something I don't" is informational.

5Obedience

Obedience is changing behavior at the direct command of an authority figure. Conformity is sideways pressure from peers; obedience is top-down pressure from someone with perceived authority. After World War II, psychologists wanted to know how so many ordinary people took part in atrocities by "just following orders."

Milgram's obedience study

Stanley Milgram (1963) told participants they were helping with a "learning experiment." Each time the "learner" in the next room answered wrong, the participant was told to deliver a stronger electric shock, rising in steps to 450 volts. When participants hesitated, the experimenter calmly said, "The experiment requires that you continue." No shocks were real, but participants did not know that.

About 65 percent obeyed all the way to 450 volts, despite the learner's screams. They were not monsters: many sweated, trembled, and protested, and most kept going anyway.

At his trial, Nazi organizer Adolf Eichmann claimed he was simply obeying orders. Philosopher Hannah Arendt called this the "banality of evil": great harm often comes not from cartoon villains but from ordinary people who stop thinking and defer to authority. Yet some people resist. The lone man who stood in front of a column of tanks at Tiananmen Square in 1989 shows that defiance is possible, and like the single ally in Asch's study, one person refusing makes it easier for others to refuse.

Zimbardo: the power of the role

Philip Zimbardo's 1971 Stanford Prison study randomly assigned college students to be "guards" or "prisoners." Within days, many guards became cruel and prisoners broke down, and the planned two-week study was stopped after six days. Zimbardo's conclusion was "bad barrels, not bad apples": put ordinary people into a toxic situation and role, and the situation often wins.

A research methods caution

The Stanford Prison study has serious criticisms. There was no control group, the sample was small, Zimbardo himself acted as the prison "superintendent," and later recordings suggest some guards were coached to be tough. The AP exam still uses it to illustrate the power of roles and situations, but be ready to name its flaws. Milgram's and Zimbardo's studies also raise major ethical issues about deception and harm to participants, which is part of why today's ethics rules exist.

6Persuasion

Imagine a brand-new soda called Starlight. Ad 1 shows your favorite celebrity laughing on a beach and taking a sip. Ad 2 cites independent lab tests showing Starlight has 75 percent less sugar than the leading cola. Both might work, but through different routes.

Central route

Persuasion through the strength of the arguments and evidence. Needs an audience that is motivated and able to think it through. Slower, but produces lasting change.

The lab-test ad.

Peripheral route

Persuasion through surface cues: a celebrity, attractiveness, music, good feelings. Fast, but tends to fade.

The celebrity beach ad.

The halo effect powers a lot of peripheral persuasion: one positive trait colors judgments of unrelated traits. An attractive, confident job candidate is rated as more competent; someone who loves one product from a brand assumes everything the brand makes is great. That is why companies pay likable celebrities to stand next to their products.

7Compliance Techniques

Compliance is going along with a request. These techniques are built to make "yes" more likely.

TechniqueThe moveExample
Foot-in-the-doorSmall request first, bigger request later. Saying yes again feels consistent with who you just showed yourself to be."Can you sign this petition?" A week later: "Can you volunteer for our campaign?"
Door-in-the-faceHuge request first (refused), then a smaller one that seems like a fair compromise."Can you volunteer 10 hours a week for two years?" "No." "Could you help at one event this weekend?"
Low-ballAgree to an attractive deal, then the added costs appear. Once committed, people often stick with it."Great price!" At checkout: "That didn't include the service fees."
Reciprocity normA gift or favor creates pressure to give something back.A free sample at the night market, then feeling you should buy something.

8Don't Mix These Up

Conformity vs. obedience vs. compliance

Conformity

Pressure from peers. Nobody has to ask.

Everyone at the table orders dessert, so you do too.

Obedience

A command from an authority.

A manager orders you to stay late.

Compliance

A request, often from an equal or a stranger.

A classmate asks to borrow your notes.

The tell: ask who is applying the pressure and how. A group just being there points to conformity, an order from above to obedience, and a request to compliance.
Normative vs. informational social influence

Normative

To fit in. You privately disagree.

Saying you love the new song everyone likes, though you don't.

Informational

To be right. You think they know more.

Copying how locals buy a train ticket in a country where you can't read the signs.

The tell: ambiguous or unfamiliar situations point to informational influence. A situation where you already know the answer but go along anyway points to normative influence (Asch's lines).
Foot-in-the-door vs. door-in-the-face

Foot-in-the-door

Small, then big.

Door-in-the-face

Big (refused), then small.

The tell: the order of the requests is the entire answer. Picture a salesperson getting a foot in a door that is still open, versus having a door slammed in their face first.
Central vs. peripheral route

Central

Evidence, statistics, reasons. The audience is motivated and thinking.

Peripheral

Looks, fame, catchy music, emotion, number of arguments rather than their quality.

The tell: ask whether the audience was persuaded by the message itself or by something around the message.
Asch vs. Milgram vs. Zimbardo

Asch

Conformity to a group about line lengths.

Milgram

Obedience to an authority giving "shocks."

Zimbardo

The power of roles and situations in a mock prison.

The tell: all three show the power of the situation over personality, which links straight back to the fundamental attribution error in 4.1.

12AP Exam Traps

Watch for these
  • Conformity is sideways; obedience is top-down; compliance is a request. Scenarios will try to blur them.
  • Normative = fit in. Informational = be right. Memorize which is which.
  • Know the headline numbers: Asch, about one-third conformity on critical trials; Milgram, about 65 percent full obedience.
  • Milgram's obedience rose with a present, legitimate authority and fell with a close victim or a defiant peer.
  • Foot-in-the-door = small then big. Door-in-the-face = big then small. The order is the whole answer.
  • The halo effect is a peripheral-route tool. One good trait spills onto unrelated judgments.
  • On "apply" prompts, name the technique and point to the exact move in the scenario that proves it.
