3What an Attitude Is

An attitude is a feeling, often influenced by a belief, that predisposes you to respond in a particular way. It bundles three pieces: a belief (often a stereotype), an emotion, and a readiness to act. If you believe a dog is dangerous, you feel afraid and you cross the street.

Attitudes shape behavior, but the situation can override them. You may be firmly against bullying, and still laugh along when everyone in the group is mocking someone. Attitudes predict behavior best when:

  • outside pressures are weak,
  • the attitude is specific to the behavior (an attitude about recycling predicts recycling better than a general attitude about "the environment"), and
  • the attitude is strong and easy to bring to mind.

4Stereotypes, Prejudice, and Discrimination

From belief to feeling to action

A stereotype is a belief about a group. It can be negative or positive, false or containing a kernel of truth, and it comes from a normal mental habit: we sort the world into categories to simplify it. Prejudice (literally "prejudgment") adds emotion to that belief. Discrimination is what happens when the attitude becomes behavior.

  1. Stereotype (belief): "Elderly people are forgetful."
  2. Prejudice (attitude): feeling impatient with Jan and assuming she will forget things, because she is 85.
  3. Discrimination (behavior): refusing to hire Jan because of her age.

You can hold a prejudice without acting on it, and laws can limit discrimination without erasing prejudice. The two do not always move together.

Two tracks: explicit and implicit

Explicit attitudes

Conscious, deliberate, and reportable. What you would say you believe on a survey.

Implicit attitudes

Automatic, unconscious, knee-jerk evaluations operating below awareness.

Because implicit attitudes hide below awareness, psychologists measure them indirectly. The Implicit Association Test (IAT) measures how quickly people pair faces from different groups with positive or negative words. Many people who sincerely deny any prejudice still show faster negative pairings for some groups.

Implicit associations can shape split-second decisions. In experiments by Keith Payne (2006), participants briefly saw a Black or White face, then an object. After a Black face, they were more likely to misperceive a harmless tool as a gun. That is why researchers worry about implicit bias in fast, high-pressure decisions like policing.

The trend lines diverge

Openly stated (explicit) prejudice in North America has dropped sharply. In 1937 only about a third of Americans said they would vote for a qualified woman for president; by 2012 about 95 percent said they would. Implicit prejudice has not faded at the same pace. For the exam: explicit prejudice has decreased more than implicit prejudice.

Prejudice attaches to many targets (race, gender, class, disability, religion, sexual orientation, nationality), but the machinery is the same: categorize, stereotype, feel, act. According to the United Nations, about one in two people worldwide hold moderately or highly ageist attitudes, which makes ageism one of the most widespread and most socially accepted forms. Subtle forms matter too: microaggressions such as "Where are you really from?" can signal that someone is seen as an outsider, and their cumulative weight wears people down.

5The Roots of Prejudice

Prejudice grows from three kinds of soil. The exam may ask you to identify which kind of root a scenario shows.

RootConceptWhat it looks like
SocialSocial identity and in-group biasOur groups give us belonging and self-esteem, so we favor "us." Harmless as school spirit; harmful when it excludes "them."
Just-world phenomenon"They must have done something to deserve it." Comforting, because it means bad things will not randomly happen to me. It leads to victim-blaming.
Ingroup vs. outgroupEven random, meaningless groups trigger favoritism within minutes.
EmotionalScapegoat theoryWhen people are frustrated or afraid, blaming a less powerful group gives the anger a target and restores a sense of control. During COVID-19, Asian and Asian American people were harassed and blamed for the virus.
CognitiveCategorization and cognitive loadSorting people saves effort. When we are tired, rushed, or stressed, we lean harder on stereotypes.
Out-group homogeneity"They're all the same. We're all different."
EthnocentrismJudging other cultures by our own standards: "Our way is the normal, right way."
Other-race effectOwn-race faces are easier to recognize. It comes from exposure, not hostility, but it causes real eyewitness errors.
A group you joined one minute ago

In classic "minimal group" studies, people are split into groups on a meaningless basis, such as which of two paintings they prefer, or a coin flip. Even then, they give more rewards to their own group and rate it more positively. You do not need history, conflict, or even a real difference to trigger in-group bias. You just need a label.

6Changing Attitudes, and Why Minds Resist

Cognitive dissonance

Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger, 1957) is the discomfort we feel when our behavior clashes with our attitudes. We want our beliefs and actions to agree, so we relieve the tension in one of three ways:

  1. Change the behavior. You care about the environment but grabbed a stack of plastic bags at the market, so you start keeping a reusable bag in your backpack.
  2. Change the attitude. "Honestly, a few bags is not a big deal."
  3. Add a justifying thought. "I recycle everything at home, so it balances out."

Because the behavior has already happened, the easiest path is often to quietly change the attitude. That is how dissonance steers belief.

The classic study: the $1 liars

Festinger and Carlsmith (1959) had students do a mind-numbingly boring task, then paid them either $1 or $20 to tell the next participant it was fun. Later, the students paid $1 rated the task as more enjoyable. Why? The $20 group had a good reason for lying. The $1 group did not, so the clash between "I said it was fun" and "it was boring" was uncomfortable, and they resolved it by changing the attitude: "maybe it wasn't that bad."

Belief perseverance and confirmation bias

Belief perseverance is clinging to a belief even after the evidence that created it has been discredited. Once we build reasons for a belief, the reasons survive even when the original basis is knocked out. The remedy the exam likes: consider the opposite. Ask yourself, "What is the best case that I am wrong?"

Confirmation bias is seeking out and favoring information that supports what we already believe. It is why two people can watch the same debate, or scroll the same feed, and each walk away "proven right." It feeds belief perseverance, because we keep finding the evidence we are looking for.

How they work together

You buy a phone and believe it is the best. A credible review shows your model has worse battery life than a rival. Dissonance: the facts clash with your choice, and that feels bad. Confirmation bias: you go looking for positive reviews and reasons the test was unfair. Belief perseverance: you keep believing "still the best." That is why changing a mind takes far more than presenting the facts.

7Don't Mix These Up

Stereotype vs. prejudice vs. discrimination

Stereotype

A belief about a group.

"Gamers are antisocial."

Prejudice

An attitude: belief plus negative feeling.

Feeling irritated by gamers and assuming the worst about them.

Discrimination

A behavior toward the group.

Refusing to let a gamer join your project group.

The tell: if someone does something to a person because of their group, it is discrimination. If it only happens in someone's head, it is a stereotype or prejudice.
Just-world phenomenon vs. scapegoat theory

Just-world phenomenon

Blaming the victim for their own misfortune.

"If he's homeless, he must have made bad choices."

Scapegoat theory

Blaming an outgroup for your own problems, to vent frustration.

"We lost our jobs because of those immigrants."

The tell: just-world explains someone else's bad outcome as deserved. Scapegoating shifts blame for my group's bad outcome onto someone else.
In-group bias vs. out-group homogeneity bias

In-group bias

"We are better."

Rating your own school's team as more talented and more fair.

Out-group homogeneity

"They are all the same."

"Students from that school are all stuck-up."

The tell: in-group bias is about favoring us. Out-group homogeneity is about how varied (or not) we think they are.
Belief perseverance vs. confirmation bias

Belief perseverance

A belief survives after its evidence is discredited.

Still believing a supplement works after the study behind it was retracted.

Confirmation bias

Actively seeking and favoring evidence that fits.

Only clicking on videos that praise the supplement.

The tell: look for the word "discredited" or "debunked" for belief perseverance. Look for searching, noticing, or ignoring information for confirmation bias.
Implicit vs. explicit attitudes

Explicit

Conscious and reportable, measured by self-report (surveys, interviews).

Implicit

Automatic and unconscious, measured indirectly (reaction times on the IAT).

The tell: "sincerely says they are not biased, but..." signals implicit attitudes.

10AP Exam Traps

Watch for these
  • Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is a behavior. The exam tests this split constantly.
  • Explicit prejudice has dropped more than implicit prejudice. Know the direction of that gap.
  • Just-world = victim-blaming. Scapegoat = blame-shifting onto an outgroup. Do not swap them.
  • The other-race effect is about face recognition, not about liking or disliking a group.
  • Dissonance has three exits: change the behavior, change the attitude, or add a justifying thought. Expect a scenario asking which one.
  • Persuasion is not in 4.2. Central and peripheral routes, foot-in-the-door, and door-in-the-face are Topic 4.3.
  • On "apply" free-response prompts, say whether the scenario shows a belief, an attitude, or a behavior, and explain why using the details given.
